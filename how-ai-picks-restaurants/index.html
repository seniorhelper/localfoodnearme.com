Short answer

An AI assistant does not rank restaurants the way a search engine does. It retrieves text about restaurants and repeats what it finds, which means it favours places that are described specifically, consistently and by more than one source. It cannot taste food, see a room or read a PDF menu. BrightLocal's 2026 research found 45% of consumers used AI for local recommendations last year, up from 6% the year before, but only 2% of people who started on AI used it as their only channel and just 18% felt ready to contact the business it named.

The mechanism

It is retrieval, not ranking. That changes everything.

Does AI rank restaurants like Google does?

No. A search engine orders a list of links and shows you the order. An assistant retrieves text about restaurants and writes an answer from it, naming two or three and stopping. There is no tenth position to occupy. You are either in the answer or you are not in it, which makes the payoff far more concentrated than a ranking.

What an assistant can and cannot perceive

It cannot taste anything. Obvious, and worth saying because it explains the rest. Every judgement it makes about quality is inherited from something a human wrote.

It cannot see your photographs. The room, the plating, the light through the window at six — none of it exists to a system reading text. If the atmosphere is the reason people love your restaurant, someone has to have written that down.

It usually cannot read your menu. Menus published as images or PDFs are invisible as text. An assistant asked for the best carbonara in town cannot know you make one.

It can read specific claims. A named dish, a neighbourhood, a chef, an award, a year you opened. Concrete detail is retrievable. "Passionate about quality ingredients" is not, because it describes every restaurant and therefore none.

What it actually weighs

Specificity. Text that says something checkable gives the model something to repeat. Marketing language gives it nothing.

Corroboration. One source saying you are excellent is a claim. Several independent sources describing you consistently is closer to a fact, and models behave accordingly.

Consistency. When your hours differ between your website and your listing, that conflict is a reason to reach for a restaurant whose details agree with themselves. This is the failure mode that quietly removes good restaurants from answers.

Structure. Marked-up facts — address, cuisine, hours, price range — are unambiguous in a way prose is not. This is the least glamorous item on the page and one of the most effective.

How AI picks restaurants
The gap between use and trust

People ask AI. Then they go and check.

The adoption number gets the headlines. The verification number is the one that should change what a restaurant does.

Of consumers who began a local search on an AI tool, a vanishing 2% used it as their only channel. 43% went straight to Google afterwards. 39% went to social media. Only 18% of AI users felt ready to contact the business the assistant recommended without checking further.

Compare that with people who started on Google, where 33% simply stopped, satisfied with what they found. Starting on Google resolves the search for a third of people. Starting on AI resolves it for almost nobody.

So the assistant is functioning as a shortlisting layer bolted onto the front of the old process, not as a replacement for it. And that produces a demanding two-stage test for a restaurant: get named, then survive being looked up.

What the two-stage test means in practice

Stage one rewards being described. Written coverage, specific detail, structured facts, independent mentions. This is where most restaurants have done nothing at all, which is also why the opportunity is real.

Stage two rewards being verifiable. The moment someone types your name into Google after an assistant mentions you, everything has to agree. Hours, address, phone, whether you are actually open on a Monday.

The uncomfortable implication is that a restaurant can win stage one and lose stage two without ever knowing it happened. There is no analytics report for "was recommended, then dropped after verification".

If an AI recommends my restaurant, will I know?

Not from your own analytics. An assistant naming you in a conversation leaves no referral trail, and if the person then searches your name directly, that arrives as ordinary branded search. The practical workaround is to ask the assistants yourself: query a few of them for the kind of restaurant you run in your city and see whether you appear and whether what they say is accurate.

What actually helps

Six things, in the order they are worth doing.

None of these require a rebuild and none of them are exotic. They are unglamorous in roughly inverse proportion to how much they matter.

One

Make your facts agree with themselves

Name, address, phone, hours and category, identical on your website, Google Business Profile, Apple Maps and every directory you appear in. Conflicting details are the most common reason a restaurant fails the verification step, and this fix is free.

Two

Publish your menu as text

Not a photograph, not a PDF, not a link to a social post. Ordinary readable text on your own site. If a system cannot read what you cook, it cannot recommend you for cooking it.

Three

Write down what makes you specific

The dish people come back for. The neighbourhood. Who cooks and where they trained. What the room is like on a Friday. These are retrievable facts. "Passionate about quality" is not, because every restaurant says it.

Four

Mark up the structured facts

Restaurant schema on your own site stating cuisine, address, hours and price range removes ambiguity for every system reading you. It is a job measured in hours and it lasts for years.

Five

Get described somewhere that is not yours

Corroboration is weighted heavily. A local guide, a regional publication, an award, a genuine directory listing — anywhere independent that describes you in text. One outside source that says something specific is worth more than another page on your own site.

Six

Keep reviews recent and answer them

Reviews feed both the ranking signals and the text an assistant reads. Recency matters more than volume, and a reasonable reply to a fair complaint reads well to humans and machines alike.

What is the cheapest thing a restaurant can do to be found by AI?

Publish the menu as readable text on your own website, and make your hours identical everywhere they appear. Both are free, both take an afternoon, and between them they fix the two most common reasons a restaurant is either invisible to an assistant or dropped at the moment someone verifies it.

How does AI decide which restaurants to recommend?

By retrieving and summarising text, not by ranking places. An assistant assembles an answer from documents it can access — web pages, structured data, and in some products live search results — and names the restaurants those documents describe clearly. It has no way to assess food. What it can assess is whether a restaurant is described specifically, whether the details are consistent across sources, and whether anyone independent has written about it.

Can a restaurant pay to be recommended by ChatGPT or Gemini?

Not in the way you can pay for a Google ad. There is no auction, no bid and no placement to purchase for the recommendation itself. That is a genuine difference from paid search, and it means the way in is to be described well enough and often enough that assistants have something accurate to retrieve. Some AI products carry advertising in other parts of the interface, but the substance of a recommendation is not sold.

Do people actually trust AI restaurant recommendations?

They use it to shortlist, then verify. BrightLocal found that of consumers who started a local search on an AI tool, only 2% used it as their only channel: 43% went to Google afterwards and 39% checked social media. Only 18% of AI users felt ready to contact the business the AI recommended without further research. Adoption is fast, trust is lagging behind it.

How many people use AI to find local businesses?

45% of US consumers used an AI tool for local business recommendations in the past year, up from 6% the year before, according to BrightLocal's Local Consumer Review Survey 2026. 31% use AI for local recommendations at least monthly, and only 19% say they would not consider it. In a single recent search, 23% used an AI tool at some point.

What makes an assistant name one restaurant over another?

Being findable in text, being described specifically, and being corroborated. An assistant reaches for concrete detail — a named dish, a neighbourhood, an award, an opening year — because vague marketing language gives it nothing to repeat. It also weighs consistency: when a restaurant's hours or address differ between its own site and other sources, that is a reason to reach for a different restaurant instead.

Does my restaurant's website matter for AI, or just my Google listing?

Both, and for different reasons. The Google Business Profile carries the structured facts — address, category, hours — that assistants and search features lean on. The website is where the specific, describable detail lives: what you actually cook, who cooks it, what the room is like. A restaurant with a strong listing and a thin website tends to appear as an address rather than a recommendation.

Why can't AI read my menu?

Usually because it is a photograph or a PDF rather than text on a page. A menu published as an image is invisible as text, so an assistant asked for the best carbonara in your town cannot know you serve one. Publishing the menu as ordinary readable text on your own site is one of the cheapest and most consequential fixes available to a restaurant.

Is being recommended by AI the same as ranking on Google?

No, and conflating them causes bad decisions. Ranking is a position in a list of links, where being tenth still means being present. An assistant names two or three places and stops, so there is no tenth position — you are in the answer or absent from it. The inputs overlap heavily, but the payoff is far more concentrated.

Should a restaurant worry about this yet?

It is worth attention rather than panic. Roughly a quarter of consumers touched an AI tool during their most recent local search and almost half used one at some point in the year, so the behaviour is real and growing quickly. But the same research shows people verify what AI tells them elsewhere, which means the fundamentals — an accurate listing, a readable website, current hours, real reviews — still decide the outcome. Those fundamentals also happen to be what makes an assistant able to describe you.

What is the single most useful thing to fix?

Consistency of your basic facts across every place they appear. Name, address, phone, hours and category should match on your website, your Google Business Profile, Apple Maps and any directory you appear in. It is unglamorous, it is free, and it is the thing that most often disqualifies a restaurant at the verification step — which, given that most people verify, is the step that actually decides.
