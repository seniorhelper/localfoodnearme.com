Short answer

Your customers decide in minutes and compare three restaurants, not thirty. BrightLocal's 2026 research found 75% of consumers choose a local business in under 30 minutes, 28% decide in under five, and 72% look at three or fewer options. Food and drink is the fastest-deciding category of all. The practical consequence is that a restaurant outside the first handful of results is not being rejected, it is not being seen, and the fixes that change that are mostly free.

Which industry do customers choose fastest?

Yours. When BrightLocal ranked industries by how likely a decision was to take five minutes or less, food and drink came first, ahead of retail and home services. Professional services and trades were slowest. People are hungry, they want something quick, and they want it nearby — which is excellent news for whoever is in the shortlist and irrelevant to everyone else.

What this actually means

The instinct when business is slow is to work on the food, the menu or the room. Those matter enormously for whether someone returns. They have almost no bearing on whether someone arrives.

Arriving is decided in a window of a few minutes, on a phone, among three candidates, on the basis of whether you were visible and whether your details held up when checked. A better carbonara does not enter into it, because nobody has tasted the carbonara yet.

That is an uncomfortable thing to tell a chef and it is also the most useful thing on this page. The gap between a full room and a slow one is frequently not quality. It is presence at the moment of decision.

Website, Google, Apple Maps, any directory. If two sources disagree, a customer verifying you has a reason to pick someone else, and an AI assistant has a reason to name someone else. This is the single most common self-inflicted wound in the industry and it costs nothing to close.

Not a photograph, not a PDF, not a link to an Instagram post. Readable text on your own site. A menu that cannot be read cannot be searched, cannot be recommended by an assistant, and cannot answer the question a hungry person is actually asking.

97% of consumers read reviews before choosing a local business. A measured reply to a fair complaint does more for a reader than a perfect average does, and recency matters more than volume. Twenty minutes a week is enough.

The strategic point

Rented demand and owned demand.

Every source of customers a restaurant has falls into one of two categories, and the distinction decides how much of the margin you keep.

Rented demand is the delivery apps and the paid ads. It works, it scales, and it stops the moment you stop paying. Delivery commission commonly runs somewhere between fifteen and thirty percent depending on platform and plan, taken from a margin that was already thin. Worse, the customer belongs to the platform: you never learn who they are and cannot reach them again except by paying again.

Owned demand is your listing, your site, your reviews, your regulars, and your presence in guides and directories. Slower to build, and it does not switch off.

Neither is wrong. A restaurant with no rented demand and no owned demand is simply invisible. But a restaurant whose entire customer base is rented has no asset at all, and every year of that is a year of paying rent on customers it could have owned.

Are delivery apps worth the commission?

They can be, as incremental volume that fills a kitchen which is already staffed and already open. They are dangerous as a primary channel, because the commission comes out of an already thin margin and the customer relationship stays with the platform. The useful test is whether the orders are genuinely additional or whether you are paying a large commission on customers who would have come anyway.

How quickly do customers decide which restaurant to choose?

75% decide in under 30 minutes and 28% decide in under five, according to BrightLocal's 2026 research of 1,227 US consumers. Food and drink was the fastest-deciding category measured. Among people who were ready to act rather than browse, 32% decided in under five minutes and 22% simply contacted the first result they found.

How many restaurants am I actually competing against?

Three, in most cases. 72% of consumers look at three or fewer businesses before deciding, and only 1% look at more than ten. You are not competing with every restaurant in your city for a given diner; you are competing for one of three slots in their head, and the rest of the market is irrelevant to that decision. Whether you are in those three is decided before anyone reads your menu.

What is the single most valuable free thing I can do?

Claim and fully complete your Google Business Profile, then make its hours and phone number match your own website exactly. It costs nothing, takes an afternoon, and it is the surface most of your customers actually see. 52% of consumers start their local search on Google Search and a further 9% on Google Maps.

Do I need a website if I have a Google listing?

Yes, though not an expensive one. The listing carries the facts; the website is where anything specific about your restaurant can live in a form that search engines and AI assistants can read. A single well-written page with your menu as text, your story and your booking link does more than most restaurant websites costing thousands.

Why does my restaurant not show up when I search for it?

Usually one of three things. Personalisation, because local results are heavily weighted by the searcher's location and you are probably standing somewhere unusual relative to your customers. Relevance, because nothing on your listing or site uses the words people are typing. Or an incomplete profile that Google has little reason to surface. Search from a customer's likely location, not from behind your own bar.

Should I pay for Google Ads?

Only after the free work is done. Paying to send people to an incomplete profile, stale hours or an unreadable menu is paying to fail faster. Once your listing is complete, your details are consistent and your menu is readable, ads become a reasonable way to buy visibility you have not yet earned organically. Before that, they subsidise your competitors' verification advantage.

How much do delivery apps really cost me?

Commission on delivery orders typically runs in the region of fifteen to thirty percent depending on the platform and the plan, which is a large share of a margin that is already thin. The strategic point is not that apps are bad but that they are rented demand: the customer belongs to the platform. Anything you own — your listing, your site, your directory presence, your regulars — is demand you keep.

Is AI search something I need to deal with now?

It is worth attention. 45% of consumers used an AI tool for local recommendations in the past year, up from 6%, and 23% used one during their most recent local search. But the same research shows people verify what AI tells them, so the work that makes you visible to an assistant is largely the work that already makes you visible to a customer: readable text, consistent facts, independent mentions.

Is a directory listing actually worth anything?

It depends entirely on whether anyone reads the directory and whether the listing is accurate. A listing on a site nobody visits does little on its own, though it does contribute a consistent, corroborating description of your business in a place that is not your own website — which matters more now that assistants weigh corroboration. Our listings are free precisely because the value to us is having correct data, not a listing fee.

What should I do first if I only have one afternoon?

In this order: claim and complete your Google Business Profile, make the hours identical there and on your website, publish your menu as text rather than a PDF or photograph, and reply to your three most recent reviews. That sequence addresses the largest number of lost customers per hour spent, and none of it costs money.
